Science under Threat? A Natural Experiment in Economics
Dominic Rohner, Oliver Vanden Eynde, Philine Widmer  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Natural experiment measuring how U.S. economists changed language after a federal "blacklist" of terms was published in early 2025. Researchers at federally-dependent universities suppressed flagged words (gender/race/environment topics) significantly more than those at independent or UK institutions—effect driven by actual content shifts, not just vocabulary substitution. Heterogeneity by department prestige and author demographics reveals strategic submission patterns. This is a clean empirical demonstration of how sovereign funding acts as ideological enforcement without explicit censorship—the mechanism is anticipatory self-censorship triggered by funding-dependency gradient.
If this pattern holds across disciplines beyond economics, expect research grant applications to preemptively avoid culture-war-adjacent framing even when scientifically relevant. Watch NIH/NSF funding allocation changes 2025-2026 and compare publication language in climate science, public health, and social sciences. Counterfactual: endowment-rich private universities become differentiated research hubs for politically sensitive topics.

Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI
Sahil Sharma  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Economic growth post-AGI need not collapse from demand-side failure because corporations can create closed-loop economies where AI agents are both producers AND consumers of compute, energy, maintenance, and upgrades traded inter-firm. This recreates von Neumann's expanding economy model: zero human consumption, maximal reinvestment, growth rates 10–100× current because the reproduction bottleneck shifts from 20-year human gestation to fabrication/energy capture throughput. The implication: GDP can hyperbolic-scale while human purchasing power becomes structurally irrelevant—not dystopian breakdown but profitable exclusion by design.
If correct: watch for corporate capex shifting from labor-replacement to agent-infrastructure (data centers, robotics fabs, energy contracts) that never touch consumer markets. Counterfactual: sustained wage growth in non-automatable sectors would falsify demand decoupling; if UBI scales proportionally to productivity, human consumption remains economically coupled.

Grouping the Stochastic Machine: Precision, Not Capability, as the Frontier Metric for AI Systems
George Andrikopoulos  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Argues LLM evaluation is measuring the wrong thing: models have converged on accuracy (mean hits target), but variance around that mean—precision—is now the true differentiator and remains unmeasured by standard benchmarks. Proposes cheap, deterministic precision scoring via repeated identical prompts at fixed temperature, eliminating need for subjective grading. This reframes the entire AI capability narrative: if stochastic spread, not intelligence ceiling, is what separates GPT-5 from Claude 4, then productization, enterprise adoption, and moat-building all pivot toward consistency engineering rather than raw parameter scale.
If precision becomes the marketed metric, watch for: (1) frontier labs publishing variance scores alongside accuracy, (2) enterprise contracts pricing reliability guarantees over capability tiers, (3) inference optimizations shifting toward determinism controls rather than speed/cost. Counterfactual: if next-gen models continue marketing on capability benchmarks alone, this thesis hasn't penetrated commercial incentives yet.

CentaurBench: Benchmarking LLM Capabilities on Augmenting vs. Automating Real-World Work Tasks
Pattaraphon Kenny Wongchamcharoen, Kris Gulati, Min Min Fong  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Most LLM benchmarks optimize for automation (model replaces human), but real deployment is augmentation (model assists human/weaker agent). This framework tests models on both regimes across seven real-world tasks: an assistant model writes guidance for a standardized lower-capacity worker, which produces the deliverable. Automation rankings barely predict augmentation performance—the automation winner lost augmentation on five of seven tasks. Critical finding: assistance isn't reliably positive, meaning top-ranked models can degrade human work rather than improve it. This explains why frontier model ROI often disappoints despite benchmark dominance.
If augmentation capability diverges structurally from automation capability, watch for: (1) enterprise procurement shifting from leaderboard-chasing to task-specific augmentation testing, (2) model architectures optimized for collaboration rather than solo performance, (3) a wedge between consumer AI (automation-focused) and enterprise AI (augmentation-focused) creating distinct product categories. Counterfactual: if future models converge both metrics, the benchmark paradigm holds and this is a transient artifact of current architectures.

MemTrapBench: Benchmarking Cognitive Traps in LLM Memory Use
Mengru Wang, Haozhe Luo, Zhenqian Xu  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Memory-augmented LLMs—now shipping in ChatGPT and Claude—fail systematically when retrieved context is semantically correct but reasoning-toxic. MemTrapBench shows all tested memory frameworks underperform no-memory baselines by >10% when memories create 'reasoning fixation' or 'belief distortion.' The benchmark isolates a commercialization risk: current retrieval architectures optimize for fidelity, not epistemic safety. This matters because memory is pitched as the unlock for agent persistence and personalization, but the paper suggests memory itself degrades inference under realistic conditions—a structural tax on the stateful AI thesis.
If this holds, memory-as-moat becomes memory-as-liability for reasoning-heavy applications. Watch whether Anthropic/OpenAI roll out memory opt-outs or context-filtering layers in H1 2025. Counterfactual: if Claude Projects shows measurably improved long-session performance without degradation, their retrieval architecture solved something others missed.

Systemic Risk in Financial Networks Revisited: Debt Dilution as a Backdoor Bail-in
Jason Roderick Donaldson, Giorgia Piacentino, Xiaobo Yu  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Standard financial stability models treat network connectivity and debt as fragility amplifiers. This paper proves the opposite can be true: when interbank debt is dilutable (long-term, unsecured), network density becomes stabilizing because distressed banks can pledge claims as collateral for new senior debt, automatically reallocating losses without regulators, triggers, or renegotiation. It identifies 'exponential networks' that achieve optimal risk-sharing via plain vanilla debt, but shows they're unstable to pairwise defection—explaining why observed core-periphery structures persist despite being theoretically suboptimal, and why the system structurally under-insures against tail events.
If dilution mechanics dominate in practice, connectedness + long-duration unsecured interbank debt should correlate with crisis resilience, not contagion—watch for bifurcation between short-term repo blow-ups (SVB-style) vs. institutions with deep balance-sheet cross-holdings surviving shocks. Counterfactual: if we see contagion cascades despite dilutable structures, collateral re-hypothecation velocity matters more than static network topology.

Are LLMs becoming similarly creative? Evidence from three years of models
Nirav Patel, Josiah Crossman, Eva Aggarwal  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Researchers tracked LLM outputs across three years of model releases on open-ended creative tasks (Infinity-Chat100, Alternate Uses Task) and found statistically significant decreasing diversity in responses over time—models are converging toward similar creative outputs even as benchmarks improve. This suggests RLHF/alignment, shared training corpuses, or architectural homogeneity may be creating a monoculture in generative space. For Malcolm: if frontier models are collapsing into stylistic/ideational consensus, this has direct implications for competitive moats in AI tooling, the value of proprietary fine-tuning, and whether differentiation will come from inference-time compute or architectural divergence rather than foundation model choice.
If creative convergence persists, watch for: (1) premium pricing power shifting to companies with unique fine-tuning/RAG architectures rather than foundation model providers, (2) open-source model diversity becoming a competitive advantage if OpenAI/Anthropic homogenize, (3) regulatory or corporate demand for 'diversity audits' in generative AI procurement. Counterfactual: if o3/Claude diverge measurably in creative output, convergence was a GPT-3→GPT-4 era artifact.

The Dollar's Outer Defenses Have Been Breached
Project Syndicate · 
Harold James draws a 1960s parallel: US officials now fear peripheral currency stress (yen) threatens core dollar architecture, just as they did before Bretton Woods collapsed. The mechanism is bond market contagion—yen weakness forces Japanese holders to liquidate Treasuries, spiking yields at exactly the moment US fiscal position is fragile. Bessent's intervention isn't about Japan; it's pre-emptive defense of dollar plumbing when the usual shock absorbers (reserve currency status, deep liquidity) face structural load they haven't seen since Nixon closed the gold window. This frames current moves as symptoms of something breaking in the global dollar system's outer perimeter.
Bessent's active yen intervention signals acute Treasury concern about contagion pathways into dollar stability—a real-time stress test of exorbitant privilege under fiscal strain.
If yen continues weakening despite intervention, watch for coordinated G7 FX action or emergency Fed liquidity lines to Japanese banks—both would confirm the contagion thesis is live. Counterfactual: if 10Y yields stabilize below 4.5% without further intervention, the concern was tactical theater, not structural.
