Rationalism and Divergent Sinomodernity
Palladium · 
American AI forecasters consistently misjudge Chinese capabilities because they project Western rationalist assumptions onto fundamentally divergent institutional structures. China's state-directed development follows different optimization functions than market-driven tech evolution—centralized compute allocation, talent channeling through ideological filters, and strategic patience that doesn't register in Silicon Valley planning horizons. The piece argues Western forecasters mistake China's different modernity pathway for incompetence or copying, missing how planned capacity buildup in semiconductors, data infrastructure, and applied AI creates asymmetric advantages in deployment speed once technical thresholds are crossed. For Malcolm: this is the mechanism explaining why consensus AI race framing may be structurally wrong about the competitor it's racing.
If China's institutional model enables faster scaled deployment post-breakthrough (rather than breakthrough itself), watch for: (1) Western narrative shift from 'China can't innovate' to 'unfair advantages' once parity emerges, (2) semiconductor export controls tightening as the real strategic choke point, (3) Tesla/foreign manufacturers facing pressure as China weaponizes deployment infrastructure access. Counterfactual: continued Chinese reliance on Western foundational models would validate current forecasting assumptions.

Risk Ceilings and Development Deadlines: Pacing AI under Uncertain Safety Productivity
Li Gan  ·  arXiv ·  2026
This paper proves that a regulator can only guarantee both a safety ceiling AND a development timeline if they can rule out 'weak' safety research productivity ex-ante—otherwise one promise must break. The optimal strategy ('learn-then-replay') allocates spare compute to safety research first, building a minimum knowledge stock before development begins, requiring only 3.3% more safety productivity than the theoretical floor. Fixed compute-split rules (e.g., mandating 20% safety compute) lock in timelines but not risk levels. The customer (society) pays for certainty via slower deployment, and the mechanism exposes why current voluntary commitments from frontier labs are structurally non-binding without productivity disclosure.
Published January 2025, directly engages the live regulatory debate around AI safety commitments as governments (UK AI Safety Institute, Biden EO framework) attempt to formalize enforceable constraints without stalling frontier development.
If regulatory frameworks adopt this logic, watch for: (1) mandatory safety R&D timelines *before* scaling runs begin (pre-training pauses), (2) demands for auditable safety productivity benchmarks as preconditions for compute allocation approvals, (3) frontier labs front-loading interpretability/alignment capex rather than parallelizing it with capability scaling. Counterfactual: if labs continue integrated scaling (simultaneous capability + safety), it signals either regulatory capture or belief that productivity can't be credibly measured—making enforceable ceilings impossible.
