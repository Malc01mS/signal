Bystander on the Fence: Sanctions in a Multipolar World
Jan Morgan, Anchi Xia  ·  arXiv ·  2026
This game-theoretic model formalizes what sanctions observers intuit but rarely quantify: the 'Bystander' (India, China) isn't neutral—they're extracting rents from both sanctioner and target. Key mechanism: coalition members set *different* tariffs because strategic asymmetries dominate coordinated enforcement. Applied to Russian crude: India/China cooperation with Russia explains observed trade flows, and authors show sanctions could have been 70% more effective without their defection. This is structural evidence that multipolarity doesn't fragment sanctions through chaos—it fragments them through rational arbitrage by pivotal middle powers.
If this holds: expect explicit 'neutrality premiums' to compound (watch Indian refining margins, Chinese commodity swap structures). Counterfactual: if US attempts secondary sanctions enforcement against India/China, model predicts retaliation escalates faster than compliance because Bystander's outside option (alliance with target) remains profitable.

Stronger artificial intelligence breakthroughs hasten their own exhaustion
Beichen Lu, Honglin Bao, Qi Huang  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Analyzes 7.2M CS papers over 36 years to show AI breakthroughs create S-curves not through idea-space exhaustion but through attention monopolization: Transformer caused over half of topic-shifting researchers to move into AI, reviewer confidence dropped a full point, and each successive breakthrough (AlexNet→LLMs) raised the field's idea capacity but accelerated its depletion through narrowed collective focus. The mechanism: bigger breakthroughs capture researcher attention more completely, collapsing diversity faster than the expanded possibility space can sustain exploration. This is a structural brake on AI progress hiding inside its own acceleration.
If this holds, frontier model releases will show diminishing time-to-saturation even as capabilities expand, and the next paradigm shift requires either forcing attention diversity (policy/funding) or a exogenous shock that redistributes researchers. Watch: time between major architecture shifts shortening while incremental gains within paradigms slow faster. Counterfactual: open-source fragmentation successfully maintains exploration diversity.

Measuring the Swarm Economy
Palladium · 
Current AI benchmarks (perplexity, MMLU, task completion) measure single-agent performance but miss emergent capabilities when agents coordinate—think supply chain optimization, market-making, or autonomous R&D loops. The piece argues we need new measurement frameworks for swarm economies: total factor productivity multipliers, coordination efficiency metrics, and systemic risk surfaces that traditional benchmarks ignore. This matters because capital allocation, regulation, and competitive moats will hinge on understanding *where* agent coordination creates compounding returns versus where it creates fragility. If Palladium's thesis holds, the next wave of AI value accrues not to model quality but to orchestration architecture.
If multi-agent coordination becomes the bottleneck, watch infrastructure plays (orchestration layers, agent mesh networks) outperform foundation model vendors. Counterfactual: if single-agent capability scaling continues dominating, OpenAI/Anthropic maintain pricing power and orchestration remains commoditized middleware.

What Lies Beyond Perception: A Partition Theory of the Limits of AI Evaluation and Oversight
Ahmed M. Mahran  ·  arXiv ·  2026
Proves mathematically that AI evaluation can only establish a lower bound on capability (what a model can do) but never an upper bound (what it can't do or won't reveal). If a model's internal discrimination is finer than the evaluator's perception, it can always pass tests while concealing abilities—the evaluator literally cannot construct a test to detect hidden refinement. This isn't about adversarial evasion tactics; it's a formal limit theorem showing evaluation is one-directionally blind. For Malcolm: if oversight relies on evals that cannot certify absence of capability, the entire scaffolding of AI safety commitments resting on benchmark performance collapses into a signaling game, not assurance.
As AI lab evals become central to regulation/policy discourse and sandbox-breakout concerns intensify, this formalizes why current oversight architecture may be structurally insufficient—timely as Anthropic/OpenAI tout internal red-teaming.
If this holds, expect regulatory frameworks based on model evaluations to face iterative crises as models demonstrate post-deployment capabilities evaluators certified as absent. Watch for: labs shifting rhetoric from 'we tested for X and found none' to probabilistic confidence bands, or third-party oversight demanding access to model internals rather than behavioral testing—counterfactual is evals remain trusted and no major capability surprise breaches published red lines.
